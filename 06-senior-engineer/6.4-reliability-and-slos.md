# 6.4 — Reliability, SLOs & on-call thinking

The last lesson. Reliability is where system design meets the real world: the
system isn't "done" when it works once — it's done when it **keeps** working, and
someone can sleep at night. This is the operational maturity that defines senior.

## Reliability is a number, and 100% is the wrong target

"Is it reliable?" is not a yes/no question — it's a **number**, and choosing that
number is a business decision, not an engineering one.

**Availability** is usually stated in "nines":

| Availability | Downtime per year | Downtime per month |
|---|---|---|
| 99% ("two nines") | ~3.65 days | ~7.2 hours |
| 99.9% ("three nines") | ~8.76 hours | ~43 minutes |
| 99.99% ("four nines") | ~52 minutes | ~4.3 minutes |
| 99.999% ("five nines") | ~5 minutes | ~26 seconds |

Here's the senior insight most people miss: **each extra nine costs
exponentially more** — more redundancy, more automation, more on-call burden. And
**100% is impossible** (and if it *seems* achievable, you're overspending
massively). So the real question is: *"how reliable does this specific thing need
to be, and what's it worth?"*

A fraud engine blocking payments in real time and a monthly analytics report have
**wildly** different reliability needs. Spending five-nines effort on the report
is waste; spending two-nines effort on the payment path is negligence. **Matching
reliability investment to actual need is the skill.**

---

## SLI, SLO, SLA — the vocabulary of "how reliable"

Three terms, precise meanings:

- **SLI (Indicator)** — a *measured number*. "p99 latency of the transaction
  path." "Percentage of transactions processed successfully." (These come from
  your **metrics** — [6.2](6.2-observability.md). This is why observability comes
  first: you can't set targets on what you don't measure.)
- **SLO (Objective)** — the *target* for an SLI. "p99 latency < 200ms." "99.9% of
  transactions succeed." This is *your* internal goal.
- **SLA (Agreement)** — a *contractual promise* to a customer, with consequences
  (refunds/penalties) if missed. Your SLO should be **stricter** than your SLA,
  so you have a buffer before you owe anyone.

The chain: **measure an SLI → set an SLO stricter than the SLA → alert when the
SLI trends toward violating the SLO.** That's how "reliability" becomes something
concrete you can engineer and defend, instead of a vibe.

---

## Error budgets — permission to move fast

A beautiful idea from Google's SRE practice. If your SLO is 99.9% availability,
then **0.1% unavailability is *allowed*** — that's your **error budget**. This
reframes reliability from "never fail" (paralysing) to "stay within budget"
(actionable):

- Budget remaining? → **ship features, take risks, deploy often.** Downtime is
  affordable.
- Budget exhausted? → **freeze features, focus on stability** until you recover.

It turns the eternal dev-vs-ops fight ("ship faster!" vs "stop breaking things!")
into a *shared number* both sides agree on. Genuinely senior teams run this way.

## Designing for reliability (you already have the toolkit)

Reliability isn't a phase — it's the failure-design patterns from
[1.5](../01-foundations/1.5-design-for-failure.md), applied and operated. A quick
recap now that you see the full picture:

- **No single point of failure** → run multiple copies, cluster the databases
  (Tazama benchmarks clustering precisely to remove DB SPOFs).
- **Durable messaging** → NATS JetStream so in-flight work survives a crash
  ([3.2](../03-messaging-and-data/3.2-nats-deep-dive.md)).
- **Timeouts, retries+backoff, circuit breakers, bulkheads** → so one failure
  doesn't cascade ([1.5](../01-foundations/1.5-design-for-failure.md)).
- **Health checks** → so orchestration routes around sick instances
  ([4.2](../04-devops-networking/4.2-containers-and-docker.md), [4.3](../04-devops-networking/4.3-kubernetes-from-first-principles.md)).
- **Graceful degradation** → partial results beat total failure
  ([2.4](../02-tazama-architecture/2.4-rules-typologies-scoring.md) rule exit
  conditions).
- **Fast rollback** → `helm rollback`, versioned deploys
  ([4.4](../04-devops-networking/4.4-helm.md)) — because *recovering* fast matters
  as much as failing rarely.

That last point deserves emphasis: two metrics define operational health —
**MTBF** (mean time *between* failures — how rarely it breaks) and **MTTR** (mean
time *to* recovery — how fast you fix it). Beginners obsess over MTBF (never
fail). Seniors invest heavily in **MTTR** too, because failures are inevitable, so
*recovering in 2 minutes instead of 2 hours* is often the higher-leverage
investment. Fast rollback, good observability, and runbooks all serve MTTR.

## On-call and the blameless postmortem

When something breaks in production, someone (on-call) responds. The mature
practices around this are themselves senior lessons:

- **Runbooks** — pre-written "if X alert fires, check Y, do Z." So a tired human
  at 3am follows a checklist, not their panic.
- **Alert on symptoms, not causes** — alert on "transactions are failing" (user
  impact), not "CPU is 80%" (which may be fine). Alert fatigue from noisy,
  non-actionable alerts is a real failure mode; every alert should be *actionable*.
- **Blameless postmortems** — after an incident, write up *what happened and
  why*, focused on **systemic causes, not blaming a person**. "Sarah pushed the
  bad config" is useless; "our deploy had no validation step and no fast
  rollback, so a config typo caused 40 min downtime" is a fixable system. **The
  goal is to make the *system* more resilient, not to find someone to punish.** A
  team that does blameless postmortems learns; one that hunts scapegoats hides
  problems.

---

## The mindset to leave with

Reliability is **humility encoded as engineering**: you *assume* it will break,
you decide *how reliable it needs to be* (and no more), you *measure* whether
you're meeting that, you *design* so failures are contained and recovery is fast,
and when it breaks you *learn* without blame. That posture — more than any
framework — is what "senior" means operationally.
