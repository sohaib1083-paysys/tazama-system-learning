# 6.1 — The senior mindset: tradeoffs, not answers

## What actually changes from mid to senior

It is not that seniors know more APIs. The shift is this:

> A mid-level engineer asks **"what's the right way to do this?"**
> A senior engineer asks **"what are we optimizing for, and what will we give up
> to get it?"**

There is almost never a "right way" in system design — there are **positions on
tradeoff curves**, chosen deliberately for a context. Everything in Modules 1–5
was secretly this lesson. Let's make it explicit.

---

## The tradeoffs you've already seen (name them)

Go back through what you learned and notice every one was a tradeoff, not a fact:

| Tradeoff | One side | Other side | Where in Tazama |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Consistency ↔ Availability** | Always correct | Always up | Fraud decision vs cached network map ([1.4](../01-foundations/1.4-state-the-hard-problem.md)) |
| **Latency ↔ Throughput** | Fast per request | Many per second | Batching vs real-time ([1.2](../01-foundations/1.2-latency-throughput-numbers.md)) |
| **Coupling ↔ Simplicity** | Many decoupled services | One simple program | Microservices vs monolith ([1.3](../01-foundations/1.3-coupling-and-services.md)) |
| **Sync ↔ Async** | Simple, inline answer | Resilient, decoupled | HTTP edges vs NATS guts ([3.1](../03-messaging-and-data/3.1-messaging-models.md)) |
| **Fresh ↔ Fast/cheap** | Always current data | Cached, maybe stale | Cache TTL choice ([3.4](../03-messaging-and-data/3.4-caching-and-redis.md)) |
| **Explainable ↔ Powerful** | Auditable rules | Learned ML patterns | Core rules vs edge ML ([5.1](../05-ai-and-ml/5.1-ml-in-systems.md)) |
| **Flexible ↔ Simple** | Config-driven | Hardcoded | Data-driven rules ([2.4](../02-tazama-architecture/2.4-rules-typologies-scoring.md)) |

Seniority is *fluency in these curves* — recognizing which one you're on, and
picking your point on purpose rather than by accident or fashion.

## Tazama's design principles ARE a tradeoff philosophy

This is why I keep pointing you at
[`../../Guides/tazama-design-principles.md`](https://github.com/tazama-lf/docs/blob/main/Guides/tazama-design-principles.md).
Read it again with fresh eyes — every principle is a *stated position on a
tradeoff*:

- **"Open Source First"** → gives up some best-of-breed proprietary tools to gain
  freedom from licensing risk. A tradeoff, chosen for their mission.
- **"Do Not Reinvent a Serviceable Wheel"** → gives up custom control to gain
  reliability and speed (use NATS, don't build a message bus). And crucially:
  **"abstract third-party tools so we can swap them"** — you saw that literally
  pay off in the ArangoDB→PostgreSQL migration ([3.3](../03-messaging-and-data/3.3-databases.md)).
- **"High Performance at Low TCO"** → gives up "just throw hardware at it" to
  serve financially-vulnerable users affordably. This *is* their optimization
  target, and it explains a hundred smaller choices (caching, protobuf,
  benchmarking).
- **"Keep it Simple to Keep it Inclusive"** → deliberately gives up cleverness to
  gain accessibility for smaller operators. Simplicity as a *feature*.
- **"Design for Failure"** → gives up the fiction that things stay up, to gain
  real resilience ([1.5](../01-foundations/1.5-design-for-failure.md)).

A team that **writes down its tradeoff philosophy** is doing senior engineering
at the organizational level. When *you* propose a design, state it the same way:
"we optimize for X, accepting cost Y." That sentence is what makes you sound —
and think — senior.

## How to reason about a decision (a repeatable method)

When you face a design choice, run this:

1. **State the goal.** What are we actually optimizing for *here*? (Latency?
   Cost? Simplicity? Auditability?) Vague goals → bad designs.
2. **List the options** — including the boring one ("do nothing / keep it in one
   service").
3. **For each, name what it buys and what it costs.** Force yourself to state the
   *cost* — every option has one; if you can't name it, you don't understand the
   option yet.
4. **Check against constraints** — regulatory, team size, deadline, existing
   skills, budget. The "best" design the team can't operate is not the best
   design.
5. **Decide, and write down *why*** — especially what you traded away. Future-you
   (and your teammates) need the *reasoning*, not just the choice.

That last step — **documenting the why** — is a senior habit that compounds.
Teams call these **ADRs** (Architecture Decision Records). Tazama's design
principles doc is essentially a set of high-level ADRs.

## The traps that keep engineers mid-level

Watch yourself for these:

- **Resume-driven design** — choosing Kubernetes/Kafka/microservices because
  they're impressive, not because the problem needs them. (Re-read the "bad
  reason to split a service" from [1.1](../01-foundations/1.1-what-is-system-design.md).)
- **Ignoring the boring option** — a single well-built service or a cron job
  often beats a distributed system. Simplicity is a feature (Tazama principle #4).
- **Optimizing the wrong thing** — shaving 2ms off a path that runs once a day
  while ignoring the p99 of the hot path ([1.2](../01-foundations/1.2-latency-throughput-numbers.md)).
- **Not stating the cost** — any proposal that lists only benefits is a red flag.
  Everything costs something.
- **Designing for imaginary scale** — building for a million users you don't have,
  paying complexity now for a benefit that may never come. (Build for ~10× your
  current load, re-evaluate as you grow.)
