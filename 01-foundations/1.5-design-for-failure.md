# 1.5 — Design for failure (the mindset that separates senior from mid)

## The mindset shift

A junior engineer asks "how do I make this work?" A senior engineer also asks
**"how does this fail, and what happens when it does?"** In a distributed system
this isn't pessimism — it's arithmetic.

If one service is up 99.9% of the time (down ~43 minutes/month), and a request
must pass through **6 services in a chain**, the chance *all six* are up for a
given request is:

$$
0.999^6 \approx 0.994
$$

You just went from "three nines" to "two nines" — ~4 hours of downtime a month
— *without any single service getting worse*. **Chained dependencies multiply
failure.** This is why "design for failure" is one of Tazama's explicit written
design principles, not an afterthought.

---

## The failure modes you must design for

In a distributed system, assume all of these *will* happen:

1. **A service crashes** mid-request.
2. **The network drops or delays** a message.
3. **A downstream service is slow** (worse than down — it ties up your resources
   while you wait).
4. **A message is delivered twice** (retries).
5. **A dependency is overloaded** and failing some requests.
6. **A whole machine / zone disappears.**

Senior engineering is having a *named pattern* for each. Here they are, mapped
to how Tazama handles them.

---

## Pattern 1: Decouple with a durable message bus (absorb failure)

If the Event Director called each rule over HTTP and a rule was down, the
request would **fail immediately**. Instead, Tazama publishes to a **NATS
JetStream** subject. JetStream **persists** the message. If the rule executer is
down or restarting, the message **waits on the bus** and is delivered when it
comes back.

> The message bus turns "downstream is down → I fail" into "downstream is down →
> the work waits." That's converting a **hard failure** into a **delay**, which
> is almost always the better outcome for a fraud pipeline.

This is the single biggest failure-design decision in Tazama, and you now
understand *why* the architecture is message-driven rather than a chain of HTTP
calls. (See the messaging module 3.2 for JetStream specifics.)

---

## Pattern 2 — Timeouts (never wait forever)

The worst failure is the **slow** dependency. If you call something and wait
with no timeout, and it hangs, **your** resources (threads, connections, memory)
pile up until *you* fall over too. A slow dependency takes down its callers.

**Rule: every call across a boundary has a timeout.** A request that's taken
10× too long is, for practical purposes, failed — treat it as failed and move
on. This is why Tazama's stores and clients are configured with timeouts, and
why rule executers record `prcgTm` (processing time) — you can't manage what you
don't measure.

---

## Pattern 3 — Retries with backoff (+ idempotency)

Transient failures (a blip, a brief overload) succeed if you just try again. But
naive retries are dangerous: if a service is overloaded and everyone retries
immediately, you create a **retry storm** that keeps it down (a "thundering
herd"). So:

- Retry, but with **exponential backoff** (wait 1s, then 2s, then 4s…).
- Add **jitter** (randomize the wait) so clients don't all retry in sync.
- Cap the number of retries; then give up to a **dead-letter** path.
- And because retries mean duplicate delivery — make handling **idempotent**
  (from [1.4](1.4-state-the-hard-problem.md)).

---

## Pattern 4 — Circuit breakers (stop hammering the dead)

If a dependency has failed the last 20 calls in a row, the 21st will probably
fail too — so stop calling it for a while. A **circuit breaker** "opens" after N
failures, fails fast for a cooldown, then "half-opens" to test if it's back.
This protects both you (you're not blocked waiting) and it (you're not piling on
while it's trying to recover).

---

## Pattern 5 — Bulkheads (isolate the blast radius)

Named after a ship's watertight compartments: if one floods, the ship doesn't
sink. In software: **isolate resources so one overloaded part can't consume
everything.** Tazama's "one rule = one deployment" is a natural bulkhead — a
runaway or crashing rule-011 processor doesn't starve rule-074. The pipeline
degrades (that one rule's results are late) instead of collapsing.

---

## Pattern 6 — Graceful degradation (partial is better than nothing)

When something's down, do the reduced-but-useful thing rather than erroring out.
If one rule processor is down, the typology can still score with the rules it
*did* get (perhaps flagged as incomplete) rather than dropping the whole
transaction. **A senior system has a sensible answer to "what do we do with less
than everything?"**

---

## The overarching principle: no single point of failure (SPOF)

Walk the whole system and ask: *"if this one thing dies, does everything stop?"*
Anything that answers "yes" is a **SPOF** and needs redundancy:

- One copy of a stateless service? → run several (trivial, from [1.4](1.4-state-the-hard-problem.md)).
- One database instance? → replicate it (this is why Tazama benchmarks
  clustering — see [1.2](1.2-latency-throughput-numbers.md)).
- One NATS server? → run a NATS cluster.

You won't eliminate every SPOF on day one (a single database is a common,
accepted early SPOF). The senior move is to **know where your SPOFs are** and
have made a *deliberate* choice about each, rather than being surprised by them
during an outage.
